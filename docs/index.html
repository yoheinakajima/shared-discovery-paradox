The Shared Discovery Paradox

How a one-answer rule turns better information into worse search

Yohei Nakajima · Untapped Capital · July 2026

paper (pdf) github verification script

A group can become better informed and less likely to discover what it is looking for. The contradiction dissolves once you separate two jobs an institution does with information: ranking candidates, and allocating attempts. Pooling evidence improves the ranking. A one-answer rule then spends every attempt on the top of that ranking — and a portfolio of eight searches collapses into one search, repeated eight times.

This page is the paper's argument in runnable form. Every number is exact, reproduced by a verification script; nothing here is a simulation approximating a claim — the simulations exist so you can watch the exact numbers happen.

01The sixteen-box machine

Sixteen boxes, one jackpot. Eight searchers each get a noisy clue: right with probability 0.20, otherwise a uniformly random wrong box. Each searcher opens one box; the group wins if anyone opens the jackpot. Pick a protocol and run it.

02Quality up, discovery down

Define Q as the average accuracy of the actions taken and G as the probability the group finds the target. Sharing strictly improves Q. It can strictly destroy G. The two columns invert:

The gap is not mysterious. It is redundancy, exactly: NQ − G = E[(K−1)⁺], the expected number of redundant successful actions. Consensus maximizes per-action quality by making all eight actions the same action.

A coordinator who ignores the clues entirely and opens eight distinct boxes wins half the time. 0.50 > 0.3835: no information beats shared information, when the protocol compresses the actions.

03Discovery is a budget, not an answer

Give the pooled posterior a budget of L distinct actions. Consensus is the L = 1 endpoint of a frontier; seven coordinated actions already beat eight decentralized clue-followers. The recovery budget is L* = 7.

04The conformity dial only goes down

The natural compromise — let only a of the eight defer to the committee's pick — never helps. The plurality winner already sits in the private proposal set, so each adopter adds nothing and deletes their own proposal. Drag the dial:

05Can selfishness fix it?

Partially. Remove the planner, keep the pooled posterior, and let eight self-interested searchers split a unit prize among whoever opens the jackpot. Crowding dilutes the prize, so nobody wants to stand where everyone stands: the equilibrium water-fills the posterior, flooding boxes above a common value line. You watched it in the machine above — it reaches 0.5991: above consensus, below everything diversified.

The inefficiency is bounded exactly: no mixed equilibrium loses more than a factor 2 − 1/N against the optimal portfolio — and because the proof is a smoothness argument, the same guarantee covers no-regret learning dynamics: adaptive agents inherit it without ever computing an equilibrium.

One reward rule closes the gap entirely. Pay the prize only when exactly one searcher covers the target — reward the rescue, not the win — and every pure Nash equilibrium becomes the planner's portfolio. Marginal contribution turns selfish search first-best.

07It survives at scale

Not a sixteen-box curiosity. Grow the world proportionally — boxes and searchers together, evidence staying sparse — and the whole ladder converges:

The near-tie in the middle is the quotable part: information without coordination (0.547) is worth about as much as coordination without information (0.500). Only the combination reaches 0.874. And the flip side sharpens the domain: hold clue accuracy fixed while the world grows and consensus becomes consistent — the paradox is a property of sparse-evidence search. Frontier science. Pre-seed investing. Novel hypotheses.

The design lesson is one sentence: share the evidence, diversify the actions.
